Expertise 02 · Systemic Risk Analysis

A disruption's real cost is rarely the disruption itself.

A static risk register scores a threat once — likelihood times impact — and freezes it. It can't say whether a given disruption stays contained at its point of origin or whether the network's own structure turns it into something much larger. That second question is what systemic risk analysis actually answers.

Most risk processes stop at the first question — how likely is this, how bad if it happens — score it, and move on. That's enough for a compliance checkbox. It isn't enough to know whether the organization is looking at a bad week or a bad quarter, which is the number that actually changes what's worth spending to prevent it, and how much buffer, reroute capacity, or alternate sourcing is actually worth carrying.

A risk is systemic when its damage isn't proportional to its trigger — when a small, local disruption propagates through the network's own feedback, delay, and capacity constraints into a much larger outcome. A single late shipment is rarely the problem; a single late shipment that trips a safety-stock policy, which trips a re-order surge, which trips a capacity constraint at the next tier up, is. The trigger is small. The structure decides how far it travels.

This is a genuinely different question from the one a probability-weighted heat map answers. Scoring "supplier default: medium likelihood, high impact" tells you almost nothing about whether your network absorbs that default in a week or cascades from it for a quarter — that depends on reroute capacity, buffer inventory, alternate-sourcing lead time, and how fast the organization actually perceives and responds to the compromise, none of which a static score captures.

The practical version of this: model the propagation pathway explicitly (feedback loops and delay, the same toolkit as system dynamics), map it against real network topology (which nodes and lanes actually carry the exposure), and — where the risk has an adversarial or attack-response character — look for a genuine threshold, a frontier in parameter space beyond which containment stops working, rather than a single "risk score" that implies the danger scales smoothly.

Reference
Core question
Does this risk stay local, or does the network's own structure amplify it into something bigger — and if so, where's the threshold?
Business value
A concrete answer to how far a disruption actually travels — what reroute capacity, buffer sizing, and resilience spend are actually worth — backed by a number, not a color on a heat map.
Core method
System dynamics propagation modeling (feedback, delay, capacity thresholds) mapped against real network topology, rather than a static likelihood-times-impact score.
Where it shows up here
The Global Disruption Atlas plots live-style disruption signals directly on network topology with reroute/buffer/alternate-source scenario overlays. The DDRA model identifies a "Dynamic Inadequacy Threshold" — the detection-delay/attack-speed frontier beyond which containment simply arrives too late, a genuinely systemic (not just probabilistic) result.
