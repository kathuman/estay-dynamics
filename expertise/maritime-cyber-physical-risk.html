Expertise 05 · Maritime and cyber-physical risk

A cyber incident on a ship or in a port can become a physical loss.

Whether it does depends on how fast it is detected and contained. Owners, operators and insurers need to know which detection and response times are fast enough.

When this approach is needed

Typical business situations
Case 1

Cyber resilience of shipboard systems

Ballast, propulsion and navigation systems are networked, and class rules on cyber resilience (IACS UR E26 and E27) apply to new ships.

DecisionWhich systems could turn a compromise into a stability or safety event, and how quickly?

Case 2

Setting detection and response requirements

Investment in monitoring and incident response needs a performance target.

DecisionHow fast must detection and containment be to stay ahead of an attack's propagation?

Case 3

Quantifying exposure for insurers and owners

Underwriters and owners need more than a checklist of vulnerabilities.

DecisionUnder which attack speeds and response delays does a loss occur, and how often?

Case 4

Incident-response exercises

Crews and shore teams train against static scenarios.

DecisionHow does the incident unfold over time under different responses — and what does the team learn from running it?

What you receive
  • A dynamic model of the attack–defence race for the system in question
  • The threshold — detection delay against attack speed — beyond which containment arrives too late
  • Detection and response requirements derived from that threshold
  • A scenario set for exercises and for reporting to management and class
How an engagement runs
  1. ScopeSelect the systems whose compromise could have physical consequences.
  2. ModelRepresent attacker propagation, the defender's delayed perception, response capacity and the physical process.
  3. AnalyseMap the threshold between containment and loss across attack speeds and response delays.
  4. ApplyTranslate the threshold into requirements, investment priorities and exercise scenarios.
Reference
Approach
Ships, ports and other operational-technology environments are now networked. The relevant question is no longer only whether a system can be compromised, but whether detection and response are fast enough to prevent a physical consequence. Dynamic models answer that question, and turn it into concrete requirements for detection and response.
Core question
Is the organisation's detection and response fast enough to stop a cyber incident before it becomes a physical loss — and if not, by how much?
Method
System dynamics models of attack propagation, perception delay, response capacity and the physical process, with threshold analysis across attack and response parameters.
Evidence
The Dynamic Cyber-Risk Model (DDRA) models a cyberattack on a ship's ballast control system and identifies a Dynamic Inadequacy Threshold beyond which containment arrives too late; it runs the same equations as the paper on SSRN.
