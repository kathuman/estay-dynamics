Expertise 01 · System Dynamics

A diagnosis that holds, no matter who's in the seat.

Most operational problems get diagnosed — and re-diagnosed — at the level of the event: the late shipment, the missed forecast, the supplier that let you down. Each fix works for a while, then the same problem resurfaces in a different costume, because the fix touched the symptom, not the structure producing it.

The business cost of that pattern is bigger than any single incident: repeated firefighting, a team that's stopped trusting its own root-cause process, and capital spent on fixes that don't hold. What actually breaks the cycle is finding the structure — the delay, the feedback loop, the capacity constraint — that would keep producing a failure shaped like this one regardless of who's operating the system, and changing that instead.

That's a structural diagnosis rather than a behavioral one. "The planner over-ordered" suggests a behavioral fix — retrain the planner — that the next planner in the seat will eventually re-discover doesn't hold. "The order-to-delivery delay is too long relative to how fast demand actually moves" suggests a structural fix that holds regardless of who's operating the system.

System dynamics, in the Forrester–Sterman tradition, is the formal method for finding that structure: it models a system as stocks (accumulations — inventory, backlog, trust, attacker foothold) connected by flows, with feedback loops that either reinforce a trend or push back against it. The textbook demonstration is the bullwhip effect: a small change in end-customer demand, amplified stage by stage up a supply chain, not because anyone made a bad decision but because the structure — delayed information, order-batching, safety-stock behavior — reliably produces that amplification regardless of who's operating it.

Three methods do the actual work: causal loop diagrams to map the feedback qualitatively and get a team looking at the same structure; stock-flow simulation to test that structure quantitatively against real numbers and reference modes, not just a hand-wave; and group model building — building the diagram and the model together with the people who have to live with the diagnosis, facilitated rather than delivered, so the result is a shared model the team owns, not a slide deck they're asked to accept.

Reference
Core question
What structure — not which event — produces this recurring pattern, and would a different trigger have produced a similarly-shaped failure?
Business value
A fix that holds regardless of who's operating the system, and a model the team actually agrees on — instead of a diagnosis that gets quietly re-litigated every time the problem resurfaces.
Core method
Causal loop diagrams (qualitative), stock-flow simulation (quantitative), group model building (facilitated, built with the team rather than for them).
Where it shows up here
The bullwhip loop diagrammed on the homepage is this technique in miniature. The Dynamic Cyber-Risk Model (DDRA) is a full five-stock system dynamics model of an attack-defence loop in a cyber-physical ship ballast system — live, in your browser.
