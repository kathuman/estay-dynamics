Risk distribution (Monte Carlo)

Real disruptions don't come with a known end date. This samples each selected event's duration from its range and the freight-rate shock from ±40%, and re-costs the plan each time.

Value of flexibility

Every combination of the six levers, scored over the whole event library with the time-phased simulation: expected annual loss (each event's likelihood × its average cost over six duration quantiles) plus the annual cost of holding the options. The best portfolio minimises the sum — the real-options view of resilience.

Method, assumptions & limits

Routing. Ships follow a hand-built graph of 87 sea-lane waypoints and 119 lane segments through the world's chokepoints (Dijkstra shortest path). Distances are within ~6% of published port-to-port figures. Closing a chokepoint reroutes every service that used it; ultra-large Asia–Europe ships are kept out of the Panama Canal.

Re-planning. Each week's flow from factories through ports and ocean services to DCs is solved as a min-cost flow: production premium + inland legs + ocean rate (40% of which scales with sailed distance) + disruption surcharge + the carrying cost of goods in transit. Unmet demand costs the lost-sale cost, so the solver only lets demand go unmet when every alternative is dearer. A longer voyage cuts a lane's weekly capacity in proportion (same ships, longer round trip).

Time-phased simulation (v3). Every scenario is simulated day by day. Each disruption ramps up, peaks and fades on a profile for its type, and freight rates rise and then decay with a half-life. Planners see conditions about a week late (the control-tower lever shortens this to two days), wait out disruptions they expect to end within a week, and re-plan weekly with the min-cost flow. Ships already at sea divert mid-voyage when their route is abandoned; at a closed canal they queue, and the queue clears at +120% of normal flow (calibrated to the Ever Given). Ports clear backlogs at +15% (calibrated to the 2024 East Coast strike). When supply moves onto a slower route, the old route keeps running until the new pipeline fills; after a shortfall, extra orders rebuild stock at up to 20% of daily demand. All of these are editable under Assumptions.

Time-to-survive vs time-to-recover. After Simchi-Levi et al. (Harvard Business Review, 2014; Production and Operations Management, 2018). A day-by-day inventory simulation at each DC: cargo on lanes that stay open keeps arriving; rerouted lanes arrive late by the extra sailing time; newly used lanes deliver only after their full transit (plus ramp-up for a standby supplier). Time-to-survive is the first day demand goes unmet; time-to-recover is the disruption's duration.

Costs. Extra operating cost per TEU served × volume × duration, plus lost sales × the cost of a lost sale, plus any air freight used to plug gaps. Lever costs are annual premiums, shown separately.

Limits. One aggregate commodity (TEU); weekly steady-state flows; cargo already at sea on a cut lane is not delivered during the disruption; recovery is immediate when it ends; event effect sizes are calibrated to the public record but are assumptions. The sample company is fictional. Treat outputs as a structured way to compare options, not a forecast.

Data.

Live alerts. A hazard alert within reach of the network's ports or factories becomes a scenario using a per-type template (cyclone, earthquake by magnitude, flood, drought, volcano, wildfire) defined in data.js; PortWatch's own list of affected ports is used when it has one. Droughts affect the Panama Canal only when GDACS lists Panama.
